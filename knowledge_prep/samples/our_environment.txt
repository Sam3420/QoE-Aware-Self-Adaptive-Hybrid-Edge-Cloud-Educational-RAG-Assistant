Our Environment. The environment includes the physical surroundings as well as the living organisms that interact with one another. All the interacting organisms in an area, together with the non-living constituents of the environment, form an ecosystem. An ecosystem consists of biotic components, which are living organisms, and abiotic components, which are physical factors such as temperature, rainfall, wind, soil and minerals. Forests, ponds and lakes are natural ecosystems, while gardens and crop fields are human-made (artificial) ecosystems.

Producers, consumers and decomposers. Organisms can be grouped by the manner in which they obtain their sustenance. All green plants and certain blue-green algae, which can produce their own food by photosynthesis, are called producers. Organisms that depend on the producers, directly or indirectly, for sustenance are called consumers; they can be classed as herbivores, carnivores, omnivores and parasites. Microorganisms such as bacteria and fungi break down the dead remains and waste products of organisms; they are called decomposers. Decomposers break complex organic substances into simple inorganic substances that go into the soil and are used up once more by plants, which is why they are essential for replenishing the soil.

Food chains and trophic levels. A series of organisms feeding on one another is called a food chain, for example grass, deer and lion, or grass, grasshopper, frog, snake and eagle. Each step or level of the food chain forms a trophic level. Autotrophs or producers are at the first trophic level; they fix solar energy and make it available for heterotrophs. Herbivores or primary consumers come at the second level, small carnivores or secondary consumers at the third, and larger carnivores or tertiary consumers at the fourth.

Flow of energy. Green plants in a terrestrial ecosystem capture about one percent of the energy of sunlight that falls on their leaves and convert it into food energy. When green plants are eaten by primary consumers, a great deal of energy is lost as heat to the environment, some is used for digestion, other body work, growth and reproduction. On average, about ten percent of the organic matter of each trophic level reaches the next level of consumers; this is known as the ten percent law. Since so little energy is available for the next level, food chains generally consist of only three or four trophic levels, and there are generally a greater number of individuals at the lower trophic levels, the greatest number being producers. The flow of energy is unidirectional: the energy captured by autotrophs does not revert to the solar input, and energy moving to herbivores does not come back to autotrophs. In nature, feeding relationships are interconnected rather than isolated, forming a network of food chains called a food web.

Biological magnification. Pesticides and other chemicals used to protect crops are washed down into the soil or water bodies, and from there they are absorbed by plants along with water and minerals and enter the food chain. Because these chemicals are not degradable, they accumulate progressively at each trophic level. This increase in the concentration of harmful chemicals at successive trophic levels is called biological magnification. Since human beings occupy the top level in any food chain, the maximum concentration of these chemicals gets accumulated in our bodies. This is why food grains such as wheat and rice, vegetables, fruits and even meat contain varying amounts of pesticide residues.

The ozone layer. Ozone (O3) is a molecule formed by three atoms of oxygen. While ordinary oxygen (O2) is essential for all aerobic life, ozone is a deadly poison at ground level. However, at the higher levels of the atmosphere, ozone performs an essential function: it shields the surface of the earth from ultraviolet (UV) radiation from the sun, which is highly damaging to organisms and can cause skin cancer in human beings. Ozone at higher levels is a product of UV radiation acting on oxygen: the high-energy UV radiation splits some oxygen molecules into free oxygen atoms, which then combine with oxygen molecules to form ozone. The amount of ozone in the atmosphere began to drop sharply in the 1980s. This decrease has been linked to synthetic chemicals such as chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), used as refrigerants and in fire extinguishers. In 1987, the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) succeeded in forging an agreement to freeze CFC production at 1986 levels, and it is now mandatory for manufacturing companies worldwide to make CFC-free refrigerators.

Managing the garbage we produce. Improvements in our lifestyle have resulted in greater amounts of waste material generation, and changes in attitude, such as the use of disposable items, have added to it. Substances that are broken down by biological processes, such as the action of bacteria or other saprophytes, are said to be biodegradable; examples include vegetable peels, paper and cotton. Substances that are not broken down in this manner, such as plastics, glass and many synthetic chemicals, are said to be non-biodegradable; they may be inert and simply persist in the environment for a long time, or they may harm the various members of the ecosystem. Kulhads, disposable cups made of clay, were once suggested for serving tea on trains, but making them on a large scale would result in the loss of fertile top soil. We should therefore reduce waste, reuse materials where possible, segregate biodegradable and non-biodegradable waste, and recycle.

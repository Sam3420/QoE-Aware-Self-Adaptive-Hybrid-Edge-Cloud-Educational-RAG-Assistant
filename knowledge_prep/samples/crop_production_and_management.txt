Crop Production and Management. All living things need food, and food gives us the energy to carry out life processes. Plants make their own food, but humans and animals depend on plants directly or indirectly. To feed a large population, food must be produced regularly and on a large scale. When plants of the same kind are grown and cultivated at one place on a large scale, they are called a crop. For example, a crop of wheat means that all the plants grown in the field are wheat.

Crops are grouped by the season in which they are grown. Kharif crops are sown in the rainy season, which in India begins in June and continues to about September. Paddy (rice), maize, soyabean, groundnut and cotton are kharif crops. Rabi crops are grown in the winter season, from about October to March. Wheat, gram, pea, mustard and linseed are rabi crops. Paddy is grown in the rainy season because it needs a lot of water.

Crop production involves a series of activities called agricultural practices. They are: preparation of soil, sowing, adding manure and fertilisers, irrigation, protection from weeds, harvesting and storage.

Preparation of soil is the first step. Turning and loosening the soil is called tilling or ploughing. Loose soil lets roots go deeper, lets roots breathe easily, and helps the growth of earthworms and microbes that add humus to the soil. Ploughing is done with a plough made of wood or iron, pulled by bullocks or a tractor. A hoe is a simple tool used for removing weeds and loosening the soil. A cultivator driven by a tractor saves labour and time. After ploughing, big lumps of soil called crumbs are broken with a plank, and the field is levelled. Levelling helps in sowing and irrigation and prevents loss of soil by wind and water.

Sowing is the most important part of crop production. Good quality seeds are clean, healthy and of a good variety. A simple way to select good seeds is to put them in water: damaged seeds become hollow and light, so they float, while healthy seeds sink. Seeds are sown with a traditional funnel-shaped tool or with a seed drill attached to a tractor. A seed drill sows seeds uniformly at the right depth and distance and covers them with soil, which protects them from birds. Seeds must be sown at a proper distance so that plants get enough sunlight, water and nutrients and are not overcrowded. Some crops, such as paddy, are first grown in a nursery and the seedlings are then transplanted to the field.

Plants take nutrients from the soil, so the soil must be replenished. Manure is an organic substance obtained by the decomposition of plant and animal waste. Fertilisers are chemicals that are rich in a particular nutrient; urea, ammonium sulphate, superphosphate, potash and NPK (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium) are examples. Manure improves soil texture and its water-holding capacity and increases the number of friendly microbes, while excessive use of fertilisers can make soil less fertile and pollute water. Another way to restore fertility is to leave a field uncultivated (fallow) between crops, or to grow crops alternately, called crop rotation. Leguminous plants such as pulses have Rhizobium bacteria in their root nodules, which fix atmospheric nitrogen, so growing a legume between two cereal crops adds nitrogen back to the soil.

Supplying water to crops at regular intervals is called irrigation. Sources include wells, tube wells, ponds, lakes, rivers, dams and canals. Traditional methods use human or animal labour, such as the moat (pulley system), chain pump, dhekli and rahat (lever system). Modern methods save water. In the sprinkler system, water is sprayed through rotating nozzles like rain, which is useful on uneven land and sandy soil. In drip irrigation, water falls drop by drop directly near the roots, so very little water is wasted; it is ideal for fruit plants, gardens and trees.

Unwanted plants that grow naturally along with a crop are called weeds. They compete with the crop for water, nutrients, space and light. Removing weeds is called weeding. Weeds can be removed by hand or with a khurpi, and by tilling before sowing. Chemicals called weedicides, such as 2,4-D, are sprayed to kill weeds without harming the crop. Farmers should cover their nose and mouth while spraying these chemicals.

Cutting a crop after it is mature is called harvesting. It is done manually with a sickle or with a machine called a harvester. Separating the grain from the chaff is called threshing; a combine is a machine that harvests and threshes at the same time. Small farmers separate grain from chaff by winnowing. Harvest festivals such as Pongal, Baisakhi, Holi, Diwali, Nabanya and Bihu are celebrated in India.

Grain must be stored properly to protect it from moisture, insects, rats and microorganisms. Freshly harvested grain is dried in the sun to reduce moisture before storage. Farmers store grain in jute bags or metal bins, and dried neem leaves are used at home. Large quantities are stored in silos and granaries, where they are protected by chemical treatment.

Animals reared at home or on farms also provide food. Rearing animals on a large scale for food such as milk, eggs and meat is called animal husbandry. Animals need proper food, shelter and care.

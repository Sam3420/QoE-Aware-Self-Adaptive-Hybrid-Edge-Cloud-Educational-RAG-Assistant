Heredity. The reproductive process gives rise to new individuals that are similar but subtly different from the parents. Variations arise during reproduction. In asexual reproduction, such as in bacteria, very few differences appear between generations because they arise only from small inaccuracies in DNA copying. In sexual reproduction, even greater diversity is generated, because each offspring receives DNA from two parents. Variations accumulate across generations, and the great advantage of variation to a species is that it increases the chances of survival in a changing environment. For example, some variants of bacteria may be able to tolerate heat better; if there is a heat wave, these variants survive and the others die.

Inherited traits. The most obvious outcome of the reproductive process is that the offspring have the same basic body design as the parents, along with small variations. Rules of heredity determine how traits and characteristics are reliably inherited. In human beings, a child shows traits of both the mother and the father: for example, free or attached earlobes vary in the population, and children may resemble either parent.

Mendel's contributions. Gregor Johann Mendel worked out the main rules of inheritance using the garden pea (Pisum sativum). He used contrasting visible characters: round and wrinkled seeds, tall and short plants, white and violet flowers, and so on. He crossed tall pea plants with short pea plants. All the plants of the first generation (F1) were tall; there were no plants of medium height. He then allowed the F1 plants to self-pollinate. In the second generation (F2), not all plants were tall: about one quarter of them were short. This showed that both the tallness and shortness traits were inherited by the F1 plants, but only tallness was expressed. The trait that is expressed is called dominant, and the trait that is not expressed is called recessive. In the F2 generation, tall and short plants appeared in a ratio of about 3:1.

Genes and their copies. Mendel proposed that each trait is controlled by a factor, now called a gene, and that each sexually reproducing organism carries two copies of the factor for each trait, one from each parent. If the two copies are TT, the plant is tall; if tt, the plant is short. A plant with one copy of T and one of t (Tt) is tall, because a single copy of T is enough to make the plant tall. The germ cells carry only one copy of each gene. When the F1 plants (Tt) produce germ cells, half carry T and half carry t, and their combinations in the next generation give TT, Tt and tt in the ratio 1:2:1, so three quarters are tall.

Independent inheritance. When Mendel crossed pea plants differing in two characters, such as a tall plant with round seeds and a short plant with wrinkled seeds, all the F1 plants were tall with round seeds, so tallness and round seeds were dominant. When the F1 plants were self-pollinated, the F2 generation included some tall plants with round seeds and some short plants with wrinkled seeds, but also new mixtures: tall plants with wrinkled seeds and short plants with round seeds. This showed that the tall/short trait and the round/wrinkled seed trait are inherited independently of each other. In such a dihybrid cross the four combinations appear in the ratio 9:3:3:1.

How do traits get expressed? Cellular DNA is the information source for making proteins in the cell. A section of DNA that provides information for one protein is called the gene for that protein. Plant height can depend on the amount of a particular plant hormone. The amount of the hormone made depends on the efficiency of the process for making it, which depends on an enzyme. If the gene for that enzyme has an alteration that makes the enzyme less efficient, less hormone is made and the plant is short. Genes therefore control characteristics, or traits. Both parents contribute equally to the DNA of the progeny: each germ cell has only one set of genes, carried in separate pieces of DNA called chromosomes, so when two germ cells combine, the normal number of chromosomes, with one copy from each parent, is restored.

Sex determination. Different species use very different strategies for sex determination. In some animals, such as some reptiles, the temperature at which fertilised eggs are kept determines whether the developing animals will be male or female. In snails, individuals can change sex. In human beings, the sex of the individual is largely genetically determined. Most human chromosomes are present as maternal and paternal copies, giving 22 such pairs. One pair, called the sex chromosomes, is odd: women have a perfect pair, both called X, while men have a mismatched pair, one normal-sized X and one short chromosome called Y. So women are XX and men are XY. All children inherit an X chromosome from their mother regardless of whether they are boys or girls. The sex of the child is determined by what they inherit from the father: a child who inherits an X chromosome from the father will be a girl, and one who inherits a Y chromosome from the father will be a boy.

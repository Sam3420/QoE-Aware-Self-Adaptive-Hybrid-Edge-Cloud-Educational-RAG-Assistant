How do Organisms Reproduce? Reproduction is not necessary for an individual to stay alive, but it is necessary for a species to continue. The basic event in reproduction is the creation of a DNA copy. DNA in the cell nucleus carries the information for making proteins, and the cell uses chemical reactions to build copies of its DNA. The copying is not perfectly accurate, so the copies are similar but may not be identical to the original. These variations are the basis for evolution, and they help a population survive when its environment changes, because some variants may be better suited to the new conditions.

Modes of asexual reproduction. In asexual reproduction a single parent produces new individuals. Fission: for unicellular organisms, cell division can give rise to new individuals. Amoeba splits into two halves during cell division; this is binary fission. Leishmania, which causes kala-azar, also divides by binary fission, in a definite plane related to its whip-like structure. The malarial parasite Plasmodium divides into many daughter cells at the same time; this is multiple fission. Fragmentation: in simple multicellular organisms such as Spirogyra, the filament breaks into smaller pieces, and each piece grows into a new individual.

Regeneration and budding. Many fully differentiated organisms can give rise to new individual organisms from their body parts: if Hydra or Planaria is cut into pieces, many pieces grow into complete organisms. This is regeneration; it is carried out by specialised cells that divide to make large numbers of cells, which then change into various tissues. In budding, as in Hydra and yeast, a bud develops as an outgrowth due to repeated cell division at one site; the bud develops into a tiny individual, and when fully mature, it detaches from the parent and becomes a new independent individual.

Vegetative propagation. In many plants, new plants develop from parts such as the root, stem and leaves. Layering and grafting are used to grow plants such as sugarcane, roses and grapes. Plants raised by vegetative propagation can bear flowers and fruits earlier than those produced from seeds, and the method allows propagation of plants such as banana, orange, rose and jasmine that have lost the capacity to produce seeds. All plants produced are genetically similar to the parent. Buds produced in the notches along the leaf margin of Bryophyllum fall on the soil and develop into new plants.

Spore formation. Many simple multicellular organisms reproduce by spores. The bread mould Rhizopus has thread-like structures (hyphae) and tiny blob-on-a-stick structures called sporangia, which contain cells called spores. Spores are covered by thick walls that protect them until they come into contact with a moist surface and can begin to grow.

Sexual reproduction in flowering plants. Sexual reproduction involves two individuals and the fusion of germ cells, which creates more variation. The reproductive parts of the flower are the stamens and the pistil. The stamen is the male reproductive part and produces pollen grains, which are yellowish. The pistil, in the centre of the flower, is the female reproductive part; it has three parts: the swollen bottom part is the ovary, the middle elongated part is the style, and the terminal sticky part is the stigma. The ovary contains ovules, and each ovule has an egg cell. When a flower contains either stamens or pistil it is unisexual, as in papaya and watermelon; when it contains both, it is bisexual, as in Hibiscus and mustard.

Pollination and fertilisation. The transfer of pollen grains from the stamen to the stigma is called pollination. If it happens within the same flower it is self-pollination; if pollen is transferred from one flower to another, it is cross-pollination, achieved by agents such as wind, water or animals. After the pollen lands on a suitable stigma, a tube grows out of the pollen grain and travels through the style to reach the ovule in the ovary. The male germ cell fuses with the female gamete in the ovule; this is fertilisation, and it produces the zygote. After fertilisation, the zygote divides several times to form an embryo within the ovule. The ovule develops a tough coat and is gradually converted into a seed. The ovary grows rapidly and ripens to form a fruit, while the petals, sepals, stamens, style and stigma may shrivel and fall off. The seed contains the future plant, or embryo, which develops into a seedling under appropriate conditions; this process is called germination.

Reproduction in human beings. The period of adolescence during which the reproductive tissues begin to mature is called puberty. Changes include thick hair growing in new parts of the body such as the armpits and genital area; in boys, new facial hair and a cracking voice; and in girls, breast enlargement and the beginning of menstruation. The male reproductive system consists of the testes, which produce sperms and the hormone testosterone, and a system for delivering sperms. The testes are located outside the abdominal cavity in the scrotum because sperm formation requires a lower temperature than normal body temperature. Sperms are delivered through the vas deferens, which unites with a tube coming from the urinary bladder; the urethra is a common passage for sperms and urine. Glands such as the prostate and the seminal vesicles add their secretions, making the sperms' transport easier and providing nutrition.

The female germ cells, or eggs, are made in the ovaries, which also produce some hormones. At birth a girl already has thousands of immature eggs; on reaching puberty, some start maturing, and one egg is produced every month by one of the ovaries. The egg is carried from the ovary to the womb (uterus) through a thin oviduct (fallopian tube). The uterus is a bag-like structure where the baby develops, and it opens into the vagina through the cervix. If the egg is fertilised in the oviduct, the zygote is implanted in the lining of the uterus and starts dividing. The embryo gets nutrition from the mother's blood through a special tissue called the placenta, a disc embedded in the uterine wall with villi on the embryo's side that provide a large surface area for glucose and oxygen to pass to the embryo; wastes from the embryo are removed through the placenta into the mother's blood. Development of the child inside the mother's body takes approximately nine months. If the egg is not fertilised, it lives for about one day; the thick, spongy lining of the uterus is then not needed and slowly breaks and comes out through the vagina as blood and mucus. This cycle takes place roughly every month and is called menstruation; it usually lasts for about two to eight days.

Reproductive health. Sexually transmitted diseases include bacterial infections such as gonorrhoea and syphilis, and viral infections such as warts and HIV-AIDS. Using a covering, called a condom, for the penis during sex helps prevent transmission of many of these infections. Methods to avoid pregnancy include mechanical barriers such as condoms; hormonal methods such as oral pills, which change the hormonal balance of the body so that eggs are not released; devices such as the loop or the copper-T placed in the uterus; and surgical methods, in which the vas deferens in males or the fallopian tube in females is blocked. Some people misuse techniques that reveal the sex of a foetus to carry out illegal female foeticide; for a healthy society, the male-female sex ratio must be maintained, and prenatal sex determination is prohibited by law in India.

Microorganisms: Friend and Foe. Many living organisms are so small that they cannot be seen with the naked eye. They are called microorganisms or microbes, and they can be seen with a magnifying glass or a microscope. Microorganisms are classified into four major groups: bacteria, fungi, protozoa and some algae. Viruses are also microscopic, but they are different from other microorganisms because they reproduce only inside the cells of a host organism, such as a bacterium, plant or animal.

Bacteria can be rod-shaped, spiral or round. Fungi include bread mould and Penicillium. Protozoa include Amoeba and Paramecium. Algae include Chlamydomonas and Spirogyra. Common diseases such as cold, influenza (flu) and most coughs are caused by viruses. Polio and chicken pox are also caused by viruses. Diseases such as dysentery and malaria are caused by protozoa, whereas typhoid and tuberculosis (TB) are caused by bacteria.

Microorganisms may be single-celled, like bacteria and some algae and protozoa, or multicellular, like many algae and fungi. They live in all kinds of environments, from ice-cold climates to hot springs and from deserts to marshy lands. They are also found inside the bodies of animals, including humans. Some microorganisms grow on other organisms, while others live freely.

Microorganisms are useful in many ways. They are used to prepare curd, bread and cake. Curd contains many microorganisms; the bacterium Lactobacillus promotes the formation of curd from milk. Yeast, a fungus, reproduces rapidly and produces carbon dioxide during respiration. The bubbles of this gas fill the dough and increase its volume, which is why yeast is used in baking bread, pastries and cakes. Yeast is used on a large scale for producing alcohol and wine: it is grown on natural sugars present in grains like barley, wheat and rice and in crushed fruit juices. This process of conversion of sugar into alcohol is called fermentation. Louis Pasteur discovered fermentation in 1857.

Microorganisms are used to make medicines. Medicines that kill or stop the growth of disease-causing microorganisms are called antibiotics. Streptomycin, tetracycline and erythromycin are commonly known antibiotics made from fungi and bacteria. Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin in 1929 while working on a culture of disease-causing bacteria, when he noticed that spores of a green mould stopped the growth of the bacteria. Antibiotics should be taken only on the advice of a qualified doctor and the full course must be completed. Antibiotics are not effective against cold and flu, which are caused by viruses.

When a disease-causing microbe enters our body, the body produces antibodies to fight it. The body also remembers how to fight the microbe if it enters again. A vaccine contains dead or weakened microbes; when it is introduced into a healthy body, the body produces suitable antibodies and is protected from that disease. Diseases like cholera, tuberculosis, smallpox and hepatitis can be prevented by vaccination. Edward Jenner discovered the vaccine for smallpox in 1798. Smallpox has been eradicated from the world through vaccination.

Some bacteria and blue-green algae fix nitrogen from the atmosphere and enrich the soil with nitrogen, increasing its fertility. These are called biological nitrogen fixers. Rhizobium lives in the root nodules of leguminous plants such as beans and peas. Microorganisms also clean the environment: they decompose dead organic waste of plants and animals into simple substances, which are then used again by plants. This is why microorganisms are important in making compost.

Microorganisms are also harmful in many ways. Disease-causing microorganisms are called pathogens. Diseases that spread from an infected person to a healthy person through air, water, food or physical contact are called communicable diseases; cholera, common cold, chicken pox and tuberculosis are examples. Some insects and animals act as carriers of disease-causing microbes. The housefly carries microbes from garbage and animal excreta to uncovered food. The female Anopheles mosquito carries the parasite of malaria (Plasmodium). The female Aedes mosquito acts as the carrier of the dengue virus. We can control these diseases by not letting water collect anywhere, because mosquitoes breed in stagnant water.

Anthrax is a dangerous bacterial disease of humans and cattle, and foot and mouth disease of cattle is caused by a virus. Robert Koch discovered the bacterium Bacillus anthracis, which causes anthrax, in 1876. In plants, citrus canker is caused by bacteria, rust of wheat by fungi, and yellow vein mosaic of okra (bhindi) by a virus; these diseases reduce crop yields.

Some microorganisms grow on food and produce toxic substances, so the food becomes poisonous and causes food poisoning. Food is preserved to stop the growth of microorganisms. Common methods include adding chemicals such as sodium benzoate and sodium metabisulphite, preservation with common salt (used for meat, fish, amla, raw mangoes and tamarind), preservation with sugar (used in jams, jellies and squashes, because sugar reduces moisture), and preservation with oil and vinegar (used in pickles). Heating and cooling are also used. Milk is pasteurised by heating it to about 70 degrees Celsius for 15 to 30 seconds and then suddenly chilling and storing it; this process was discovered by Louis Pasteur. Food kept at low temperatures in a refrigerator lasts longer, and storing food in airtight packets also prevents attack by microbes.

The amount of nitrogen in the atmosphere remains almost constant because of the nitrogen cycle. Nitrogen fixed by bacteria and lightning is used by plants to make proteins, which pass to animals through food. When plants and animals die, bacteria and fungi in the soil convert the nitrogenous wastes back into nitrogenous compounds, and some bacteria convert them into nitrogen gas that returns to the atmosphere.

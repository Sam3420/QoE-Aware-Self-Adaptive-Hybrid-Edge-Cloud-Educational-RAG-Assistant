Cell: Structure and Functions. All living things are made of small units called cells, just as a building is made of bricks. The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life. Robert Hooke first observed cells in 1665, when he looked at thin slices of cork under a simple magnifying device and saw small boxes like the chambers of a honeycomb. He called these boxes cells.

Organisms differ in the number of cells they have. Organisms made of only one cell are called unicellular organisms; Amoeba and Paramecium are examples. A single cell carries out all the basic functions, such as taking in food, digestion, excretion, respiration, growth and reproduction. Organisms made of more than one cell are called multicellular organisms; humans, plants and most animals are examples. In multicellular organisms, different cells are specialised to do different jobs. Even a multicellular organism starts its life as a single cell, the fertilised egg.

Cells differ in shape and size. Amoeba has no fixed shape; it keeps changing its shape by forming finger-like projections called pseudopodia, which help it move and capture food. Most cells have a definite shape related to their function. Nerve cells are long and branched so that they can receive and transfer messages, which helps to control and coordinate the working of different parts of the body. Muscle cells are spindle-shaped. Red blood cells are round and biconcave. The size of cells varies a lot. The smallest cell, found in bacteria, is about 0.1 to 0.5 micrometre, while the largest single cell is the egg of an ostrich. The size of a cell is not related to the size of the organism: the cells of an elephant are not necessarily larger than those of a rat.

A group of similar cells performing a particular function forms a tissue. Different tissues together form an organ, such as the stomach, heart or leaf, and organs work together in organ systems. Every organ is made of smaller parts, and the cell is the smallest of these working parts.

The basic components of a cell are the cell membrane, the cytoplasm and the nucleus. The cell membrane, also called the plasma membrane, encloses the cell and separates it from its surroundings. It is porous and allows the movement of selected substances into and out of the cell, so it gives shape to the cell and controls what enters and leaves. In plant cells, there is an additional thick outer layer called the cell wall, made mainly of cellulose. The cell wall gives protection and rigidity to the plant cell, which helps plants withstand changes in temperature, high wind speed and atmospheric moisture. Bacterial cells also have a cell wall.

The cytoplasm is the jelly-like substance present between the cell membrane and the nucleus. Various other components of the cell, called organelles, are present in the cytoplasm. Examples of organelles are mitochondria, the Golgi bodies and ribosomes. Mitochondria release energy from food; ribosomes help in making proteins.

The nucleus is an important component of the living cell. It is generally spherical and located in the centre of the cell. It is separated from the cytoplasm by a membrane called the nuclear membrane, which is also porous. Inside the nucleus there is a smaller spherical body called the nucleolus. The nucleus contains thread-like structures called chromosomes, which carry genes and help in the inheritance or transfer of characters from parents to offspring. Chromosomes can be seen only when the cell divides. A gene is the unit of inheritance in living organisms; it controls the transfer of a hereditary characteristic from parents to offspring. The nucleus also controls the activities of the cell, so it is called the control centre of the cell. The entire content of a living cell is known as protoplasm, which includes the cytoplasm and the nucleus; it is called the living substance of the cell.

In some organisms, such as bacteria, the nucleus is not well organised: there is no nuclear membrane, and the nuclear material is present in the cytoplasm. Such cells are called prokaryotic cells and the organisms are prokaryotes. Blue-green algae are also prokaryotes. Cells with a well-organised nucleus enclosed by a nuclear membrane are called eukaryotic cells. All organisms other than bacteria and blue-green algae are eukaryotes.

Vacuoles are clear spaces in the cytoplasm. In plant cells there is usually one large central vacuole, which stores substances and keeps the cell firm. Animal cells have small vacuoles or none. Plant cells also contain small coloured bodies called plastids. Green plastids are called chloroplasts; they contain the green pigment chlorophyll, which is essential for photosynthesis. Plastids of other colours give colour to flowers and fruits.

Plant cells and animal cells differ in several ways. A plant cell has a cell wall, plastids such as chloroplasts, and a large vacuole, while an animal cell has no cell wall, no plastids and only small vacuoles if any. Both plant and animal cells have a cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus and mitochondria. Onion peel cells and cheek cells are commonly observed under a microscope in the laboratory; stains such as iodine, methylene blue or safranin are used to make the parts of the cell clearly visible.

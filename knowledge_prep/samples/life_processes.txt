Life Processes. The processes which together perform the maintenance job in living organisms are called life processes. They include nutrition, respiration, transportation and excretion. Living organisms need energy from outside, in the form of food, to maintain their bodies, repair damage and grow. Since the food source is outside the body, organisms need nutrition; since breaking down food to release energy usually uses oxygen, they need respiration; and since wastes are produced, they need excretion.

Nutrition. Autotrophic nutrition is the mode in which organisms make their own food from simple inorganic substances such as carbon dioxide and water. Green plants and some bacteria are autotrophs. Heterotrophic organisms depend on food made by others. Photosynthesis is the process by which autotrophs take in carbon dioxide and water and convert them into carbohydrates in the presence of sunlight and chlorophyll. The overall equation is: 6CO2 + 12H2O, in the presence of chlorophyll and sunlight, gives C6H12O6 + 6O2 + 6H2O. Three events occur during photosynthesis: absorption of light energy by chlorophyll; conversion of light energy to chemical energy and splitting of water molecules into hydrogen and oxygen; and reduction of carbon dioxide to carbohydrates. The carbohydrates not used immediately are stored as starch.

Chlorophyll is present in chloroplasts, the green dots seen in leaf cells. Gaseous exchange in leaves happens through stomata, tiny pores on the surface of leaves. The opening and closing of each pore is controlled by guard cells: guard cells swell when water flows into them and the pore opens, and they shrink when they lose water and the pore closes. Plants also take up water from the soil through the roots, and nitrogen, phosphorus, iron and magnesium from the soil as minerals. Nitrogen is taken up as inorganic nitrates or nitrites, or as organic compounds made by bacteria from atmospheric nitrogen.

Heterotrophic nutrition can be holozoic, saprophytic or parasitic. Saprophytes such as fungi break down food material outside the body and absorb it. Parasites such as cuscuta (amar-bel), ticks, lice and tapeworms get nutrition from plants or animals without killing them. Amoeba takes in food using temporary finger-like extensions of the cell surface called pseudopodia, which fuse over the food particle to form a food vacuole; inside it, complex substances are broken down and diffuse into the cytoplasm. Paramoecium has a definite shape and takes in food at a specific spot, moved there by cilia covering the cell surface.

Human digestion. The alimentary canal is a long tube from the mouth to the anus. Food is crushed by the teeth and mixed with saliva; the salivary amylase enzyme breaks starch into simple sugar. The lining of the canal has muscles that contract rhythmically to push food forward; these movements are called peristalsis. The gastric glands of the stomach release hydrochloric acid, the protein-digesting enzyme pepsin, and mucus. The acid creates an acidic medium that helps pepsin act, and mucus protects the stomach lining from the acid. The sphincter muscle regulates the exit of food from the stomach into the small intestine.

The small intestine is the site of complete digestion of carbohydrates, proteins and fats. It receives bile juice from the liver and pancreatic juice from the pancreas. Bile makes the food alkaline so pancreatic enzymes can act, and bile salts break fat into small globules (emulsification). Pancreatic juice contains trypsin for digesting proteins and lipase for breaking down emulsified fats. The walls of the small intestine have glands that secrete intestinal juice, which converts proteins to amino acids, complex carbohydrates to glucose and fats to fatty acids and glycerol. The inner lining has numerous finger-like projections called villi, which increase the surface area for absorption; they are richly supplied with blood vessels. Unabsorbed food passes to the large intestine, where more villi absorb water, and the rest is removed through the anus, regulated by the anal sphincter.

Respiration. Food such as glucose is first broken down in the cytoplasm into a three-carbon molecule called pyruvate. In yeast, without oxygen, pyruvate is converted into ethanol and carbon dioxide; this is anaerobic respiration (fermentation). In the mitochondria, with oxygen, pyruvate is broken down into carbon dioxide and water, releasing much more energy; this is aerobic respiration. When there is a lack of oxygen in our muscle cells, for example during vigorous exercise, pyruvate is converted into lactic acid, and its build-up causes cramps. The energy released during respiration is used to make ATP, which acts as the energy currency of the cell: the energy stored in ATP is used for muscle contraction, protein synthesis and conduction of nerve impulses.

In humans, air is taken in through the nostrils, where fine hairs and mucus filter it. It passes through the pharynx, larynx and trachea; rings of cartilage in the throat ensure the air passage does not collapse. In the lungs, the passage divides into bronchi and then bronchioles, which end in balloon-like structures called alveoli. The alveoli provide a very large surface for exchange of gases and have an extensive network of blood vessels. When we breathe in, the ribs lift and the diaphragm flattens, enlarging the chest cavity so air is sucked into the lungs. The respiratory pigment haemoglobin, present in red blood corpuscles, has a very high affinity for oxygen and carries it to tissues. Carbon dioxide is more soluble in water, so it is mostly transported dissolved in blood.

Transportation in humans. Blood consists of a fluid medium called plasma, in which cells are suspended: red blood corpuscles carry oxygen, white blood corpuscles fight infection, and platelets help blood to clot at injuries. The heart is a muscular organ about the size of a fist with four chambers. Oxygen-rich blood from the lungs enters the left atrium, passes to the left ventricle, and is pumped to the body. Deoxygenated blood from the body enters the right atrium, passes to the right ventricle, and is pumped to the lungs. Valves ensure blood does not flow backwards. Because blood goes through the heart twice in each cycle, this is called double circulation; it keeps oxygenated and deoxygenated blood separate, which is necessary in birds and mammals with high energy needs. Arteries carry blood away from the heart and have thick, elastic walls because the blood is under high pressure. Veins collect blood from organs and bring it back to the heart; they have valves. The smallest vessels, capillaries, have walls one cell thick, through which exchange of materials takes place. Lymph, a fluid that escapes from capillaries into intercellular spaces, carries digested and absorbed fat from the intestine and drains excess fluid back into the blood.

Transportation in plants. Xylem moves water and minerals obtained from the soil, and phloem transports the products of photosynthesis from the leaves to other parts of the plant. Root cells take up ions from the soil, creating a difference in ion concentration that makes water move into the root (root pressure). Loss of water as vapour from the aerial parts of the plant is called transpiration; it creates a suction that pulls water up through the xylem from the roots, and is the major driving force for water movement in tall plants during the day. Transport of soluble products of photosynthesis is called translocation and occurs in the phloem. It uses energy from ATP: sucrose is transferred into phloem tissue, increasing the osmotic pressure so water moves in, and this pressure moves the material to tissues with less pressure.

Excretion. The biological process involved in the removal of harmful metabolic wastes from the body is called excretion. The human excretory system includes a pair of kidneys, a pair of ureters, a urinary bladder and a urethra. Each kidney has large numbers of filtration units called nephrons. In each nephron, a cup-shaped end called Bowman's capsule surrounds a cluster of very thin-walled blood capillaries called the glomerulus, where blood is filtered. Some substances in the initial filtrate, such as glucose, amino acids, salts and a major amount of water, are selectively reabsorbed as the urine flows along the tube. The urine formed in each kidney passes through the ureter into the urinary bladder, where it is stored until released through the urethra. When kidneys fail, an artificial kidney removes nitrogenous waste from the blood through dialysis. Plants get rid of excess water by transpiration and of oxygen through stomata; many plant waste products are stored in cellular vacuoles or in leaves that fall off, and other wastes are stored as resins and gums, especially in old xylem.

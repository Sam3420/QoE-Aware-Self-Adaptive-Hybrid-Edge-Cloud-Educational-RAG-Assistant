Control and Coordination. All living organisms respond to changes in their environment, such as light, heat, touch or the presence of food. In animals, control and coordination are provided by nervous and muscular tissues together with hormones. In plants, coordination is achieved by plant hormones and by responses such as tropic movements.

The nervous system. Information from the environment is detected by specialised tips of nerve cells called receptors, located in the sense organs: the inner ear, the nose, the tongue, the eyes and the skin. A neuron, or nerve cell, has three parts: dendrites, which acquire information; the cell body; and the axon, a long extension along which information travels as an electrical impulse. At the end of the axon, the electrical impulse sets off the release of chemicals, which cross a small gap called the synapse and start a similar electrical impulse in the dendrite of the next neuron. In this way the impulse travels from neuron to neuron, and finally reaches muscle or gland cells.

Reflex actions. A reflex action is a sudden, involuntary response to something in the environment, such as pulling the hand away from a flame. Nerves that detect heat are connected to nerves that move muscles, and this pathway, from the input nerve to the output nerve through the spinal cord, is called a reflex arc. Reflex arcs are formed in the spinal cord, where the input is processed, so the response is quick and does not need thinking by the brain. However, the information also reaches the brain. Reflex arcs evolved in animals because the thinking process of the brain is not fast enough.

The human brain. The brain and the spinal cord form the central nervous system, which receives information from all parts of the body and integrates it. The nerves from the brain and spinal cord to other parts form the peripheral nervous system. The brain has three major parts: the fore-brain, the mid-brain and the hind-brain. The fore-brain is the main thinking part of the brain; it has regions that receive sensory impulses from various receptors and separate areas for hearing, smell and sight, as well as areas that control voluntary movement. Involuntary actions such as blood pressure, salivation and vomiting are controlled by the medulla in the hind-brain. The cerebellum, also in the hind-brain, is responsible for precision of voluntary actions and for maintaining posture and balance, such as walking in a straight line, riding a bicycle or picking up a pencil. The brain is protected inside a bony box, the skull, and is cushioned by fluid-filled balloons; the spinal cord is protected by the vertebral column (backbone).

How nervous tissue causes action. When a nerve impulse reaches a muscle, the muscle fibre moves. Muscle cells contain special proteins that change their shape and arrangement in response to nervous electrical impulses, which makes the muscle cells shorter. This is how muscles contract and cause movement.

Coordination in plants. Plants have no nervous system or muscles, but they respond to stimuli. The leaves of the sensitive plant (Mimosa, chhui-mui) fold up and droop when touched; this movement is not growth-related and happens because plant cells change shape by changing the amount of water in them. Other movements are growth-related and directional; they are called tropisms. Shoots bend towards light (phototropism); roots grow downwards towards the pull of the earth (geotropism, or gravitropism) and towards water (hydrotropism); and pollen tubes grow towards ovules (chemotropism). The tendrils of a pea plant are sensitive to touch: the part of the tendril away from the object grows faster than the part in contact, so it coils around the support.

Plant hormones. Auxin is synthesised at the tip of the shoot. When light comes from one side, auxin diffuses towards the shady side of the shoot, which stimulates the cells there to grow longer, so the shoot bends towards light. Gibberellins help in the growth of the stem. Cytokinins promote cell division, and are present in high concentrations in areas of rapid cell division such as fruits and seeds. Abscisic acid inhibits growth; its effects include wilting of leaves.

Hormones in animals. Hormones are chemical messengers secreted by endocrine glands directly into the blood, which carries them to target organs. When an animal faces danger, the adrenal glands secrete adrenaline into the blood. Adrenaline makes the heart beat faster to supply more oxygen to the muscles, reduces blood supply to the digestive system and skin by contracting the small arteries there, and increases the breathing rate, preparing the body to deal with the situation. Iodine is necessary for the thyroid gland to make thyroxin, a hormone that regulates carbohydrate, protein and fat metabolism; a lack of iodine in the diet can cause goitre, a swollen neck, which is why iodised salt is recommended. Growth hormone, secreted by the pituitary gland, regulates growth and development; its deficiency in childhood leads to dwarfism, and excess causes gigantism. Testosterone in males and oestrogen in females bring about the changes of puberty. Insulin, produced by the pancreas, helps regulate blood sugar levels; if it is not secreted in proper amounts, blood sugar rises and causes diabetes. The timing and amount of hormone released are regulated by feedback mechanisms: for example, if sugar levels in the blood rise, the cells of the pancreas detect it and produce more insulin, and as the sugar level falls, insulin secretion is reduced.
